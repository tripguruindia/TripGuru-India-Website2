Bhutan is one of the easiest foreign trips an Indian can plan. There is no visa and no passport stamp drama. You need an Entry Permit, which is free, and you pay a daily Sustainable Development Fee (SDF) for each night you stay. Then you choose between driving in through Phuentsholing or flying into Paro, and either way you end up among prayer flags, apple orchards and mountain monasteries.

Rules change; confirm on the official Bhutan immigration and tourism pages (immi.gov.bt) before you book, or message us.

## Do Indians need a visa for Bhutan?

No. Indians do not need a visa. They need an **Entry Permit**, which is free of charge.

The permit can be collected on arrival at Phuentsholing or Paro Airport. You can also apply online through the immigration portal (immi.gov.bt). Sources suggest allowing about 5 working days if you apply online. Practice on the spot can vary, so do not leave it to chance if your dates are fixed.

## Which ID is accepted, and what documents do you need?

Indians usually travel on an Indian passport or Voter ID. Other documents, such as Aadhaar, may be accepted. Check the current list on immi.gov.bt.

For the permit, keep ready:

- A photocopy of your passport or Voter ID
- Hotel booking confirmation
- Passport-size photos

Children's ID rules differ from adults'. Check the official page or ask us before travelling with kids.

A practical habit: carry the original ID plus a couple of photocopies and keep digital copies on your phone. Permit offices and hotels often ask for copies, and mountain roads are no place to hunt for a photocopy shop.

## What is the Sustainable Development Fee for Indians?

As of November 2026, sources give the SDF for Indian nationals as **₹1,200 per person per night**.

| Traveller | SDF per night |
|---|---|
| Adult | ₹1,200 |
| Child 6–12 years | ₹600 (50%) |
| Child under 6 | Free |

Bhutan's ngultrum is pegged 1:1 to the rupee, so there is no conversion maths. A 7-night stay works out to about ₹8,400 per adult in SDF.

You can pay online with the permit application, in cash at entry ports, or through a Bhutanese tour operator. Short visits to the border towns may be treated differently for SDF. Check the official page.

## Entry permit vs route permit: where can you go?

As of November 2026, per operator sources, the Entry Permit covers **Thimphu and Paro**; confirm on immi.gov.bt. To go further, such as Punakha, Bumthang, Ha or eastern Bhutan, you need a separate free **Route Permit** from the immigration office in Thimphu, which reportedly works on weekdays (confirm on immi.gov.bt).

Plan around this. If you land on a Friday evening and want Punakha on Saturday, a weekday-only office can catch you out (confirm on immi.gov.bt). Applying early or going through an operator avoids the problem.

For the road route, the usual gateways on the Indian side are around Siliguri, Hasimara and Bagdogra. Check IRCTC or airlines for current trains, flights and fares. Book early. Message us for a quote on either option.

The road route costs less on transport but uses a day at each end. If you have only 5 days, flying usually makes more sense. With 7 or more days, the drive up from the plains is itself part of the trip.

## What is worth seeing in Paro, Thimphu and Punakha?

**Paro** is where most trips begin. The Tiger's Nest (Taktsang) monastery clings to a cliff high above the valley, and the walk up through pine forest is the one thing most visitors plan their whole trip around. In town, Rinpung Dzong and the old wooden covered bridge below it make an easy evening walk.

**Thimphu** is a small capital with no traffic lights, which surprises first-time visitors. You can see the giant Buddha Dordenma statue on the hill at Buddha Point, look in at the weekend vegetable and handicraft markets, and visit Tashichho Dzong, the seat of government. Archery, the national sport, is often played at grounds in town, and watching a match is entertaining.

**Punakha** sits lower and is warmer. Punakha Dzong stands where two rivers, the Pho Chhu and Mo Chhu, meet, and the setting is its main attraction. The drive there crosses Dochula Pass, where 108 memorial chortens stand among prayer flags. Rice terraces surround the valley below.

Dzongs and temples usually expect modest dress, and some areas restrict photography inside. Check opening days and entry fees locally, as they change.

## What does a Bhutan itinerary look like?

For 5–7 days, the classic loop is Paro, Thimphu and Punakha. This is a suggested plan rather than a fixed package.

1. **Day 1: Arrive in Paro.** Collect the permit, check in, walk through the town in the evening.
2. **Day 2: Paro.** The Tiger's Nest hike is the big day. Start early, wear proper shoes and take it slowly.
3. **Day 3: Paro to Thimphu.** Explore the capital's monasteries, markets and Buddha Point.
4. **Day 4: Thimphu to Punakha via Dochula Pass.** On a clear day the pass gives Himalayan views, with its chorten and prayer flags. Punakha needs a Route Permit (confirm on immi.gov.bt).
5. **Day 5: Punakha.** Visit the dzong at the river confluence and the surrounding rice valley.
6. **Day 6: Return towards Paro or Phuentsholing.**
7. **Day 7: Departure.**

If you are driving in, add a day at the start and end for the Phuentsholing–Thimphu stretch.

## What food should you try?

Bhutanese food is simple and chilli-forward. The national dish is ema datshi, chillies cooked in a cheese sauce, usually served with red rice. You will also find momos, noodle soups and butter tea. Vegetarians do fine, because many dishes are built around vegetables and cheese, but tell your hotel in advance if you eat no onion or garlic or follow other restrictions. Indian-style meals are widely available at tourist hotels.

## When is the best time to go?

Spring (March to May) and autumn (September to November) are the usual favourites for clear skies and festivals. Weather in the mountains changes quickly, so check conditions close to your dates. Roads can be affected by landslides in the monsoon.

Pack layers even in warm months, as mornings and evenings in the hills are cool. Bring comfortable walking shoes, a rain jacket and sun protection. If you have a heart, breathing or knee condition, consult a doctor before planning the Tiger's Nest hike.

## Who is a Bhutan trip best suited to?

Couples and honeymooners enjoy the quiet valleys and small hotels. Families with school-age children do well on the Paro–Thimphu–Punakha loop, as drives are short and the SDF for children aged 6–12 is half. Senior citizens can enjoy the towns and dzongs at an easy pace and skip the long hike. Friends' groups often add Ha or Bumthang if they have extra days, which needs the Route Permit.
