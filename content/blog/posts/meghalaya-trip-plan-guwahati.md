Meghalaya packs waterfalls, living root bridges and a river so clear that boats look like they're floating on air into a trip that works well over five or six days from Guwahati. This guide lays out a practical route — Shillong, Cherrapunji, Dawki and the villages around them — along with when to go, what it costs and the documents and local rules you need to know before you set off.

## How to reach Meghalaya from Guwahati

Guwahati is the gateway. It has an airport with regular domestic connections to the big Indian cities and is also a major railhead, so most travellers from UP, Bihar and Delhi NCR fly or take the train into Guwahati first — check IRCTC or your airline directly for current routes and timings rather than relying on old schedules.

From Guwahati, Shillong is about 100 km away on NH6, a drive of roughly 2–3 hours depending on traffic through the hill stretches. Cherrapunji (locally called Sohra) is about 148 km from Guwahati, and only around 53–54 km — roughly 2 hours — from Shillong itself.

You can do this leg by private taxi (sedans, SUVs and Innovas are all available at Guwahati airport and in the city) or by shared Sumo/cab. Shared-seat fares vary a lot between operators and seasons, so treat any number you see online as indicative and confirm the fare before you get in. A private cab for the whole circuit — Guwahati–Shillong–Cherrapunji–Dawki–Guwahati — is the easiest way to manage a 5–6 day trip with luggage, viewpoints and village detours along the way.

## Best time to visit Shillong, Cherrapunji and Dawki

October to February is the best overall window. Skies are clear, days are cool (roughly 20–25°C), and waterfalls still have reasonable flow after the monsoon. The Umngot river at Dawki is clearest roughly from November to February, once the heaviest rains have passed and sediment has settled.

March to May is a workable shoulder season: warmer, with scattered showers, and fewer tourists at the popular spots. The river is less reliably clear in this period.

June to September is the monsoon, and Cherrapunji is one of the wettest places on earth, so the waterfalls are at their most dramatic. But near-daily rain makes the Nongriat root-bridge trek slippery and harder going, the Umngot turns muddy, and hill roads such as Shillong–Cherrapunji and the routes to Dawki are more prone to landslides and blocks. If you do travel in the monsoon, build slack into your plan and check road conditions before you set out each day.

## A 5–6 day Meghalaya trip plan from Guwahati

Six days gives you a comfortable pace with one night each in Shillong, Cherrapunji and the Dawki/Shnongpdeng area, plus room for the Nongriat trek without rushing. Five days works if you're happy to see Shillong on your way through rather than as a separate base.

| Day | Route | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Guwahati → Shillong (~100 km, 2–3 hrs) | Settle in, evening walk around Police Bazar and Ward's Lake |
| 2 | Shillong | City sights by day; Laitlum Canyon (~24 km) for sunset if the weather is clear |
| 3 | Shillong → Cherrapunji (~54 km, ~2 hrs) | Mawsmai Cave, Nohkalikai Falls, Seven Sisters Falls viewpoint; overnight in Sohra |
| 4 | Cherrapunji | Full day for the Double Decker root bridge trek via Tyrna village (consider an overnight at Nongriat if you don't want to rush the climb back) |
| 5 | Cherrapunji → Dawki/Shnongpdeng, via Mawlynnong | Riwai living root bridge and Mawlynnong village, then boating on the Umngot; overnight by the river |
| 6 | Dawki → Guwahati (optional detour via Jowai/Krang Shuri Falls) | Return drive, with time for departure |

If you only have five days, fold Day 1 and Day 2 into one overnight in Shillong, or skip the overnight at Nongriat and do the root bridge trek as a long day trip from Cherrapunji instead.

## Shillong: a day or two in the hill capital

Shillong sits around Ward's Lake and the market lanes of Police Bazar, with a cooler, misty feel that comes from its altitude. It's a good base for your first day of acclimatising and for day trips to viewpoints that aren't comfortable to combine with a long drive day.

Laitlum Canyon, about 24 km out, is the standout half-day trip — Meghalaya Tourism describes it as the "end of the hills," a grassy ridge where the land drops away into gorges and rolling hills beyond. It's often foggy in the mornings, so aim for a clear afternoon and stay for sunset if the weather cooperates.

## Cherrapunji (Sohra): caves, falls and the living root bridges

Cherrapunji is the base for most of what people come to Meghalaya for. Mawsmai Cave is an easy limestone cave walk suitable for most visitors — it's generally open in the daytime, but check timings locally since hours at these smaller attractions can change. Nohkalikai Falls, one of India's tallest waterfalls, and the Seven Sisters Falls viewpoint are both close by and make for an easy half-day of sightseeing.

The big draw, though, is the Double Decker Living Root Bridge at Nongriat — a bridge grown, not built, from the roots of rubber fig trees, trained over generations into a working footbridge with a second deck above the first. Getting there is the real adventure: you drive 30–45 minutes from Sohra to Tyrna village, then descend around 3,000–3,500 stone steps over roughly 3 km, passing through Nongthymmai village and crossing a few steel suspension bridges on the way down to Nongriat.

This is a trek, not a stroll — going down is manageable for most reasonably fit visitors, but the climb back up is the hard part, and it isn't suitable for small children, older travellers or anyone with knee or heart conditions without careful thought. Many visitors who don't want to rush the return climb stay overnight at a homestay in Nongriat and walk back up the next morning.

A smaller, much easier alternative is the Riwai Living Root Bridge near Mawlynnong — about a 10-minute walk from the village, with none of the stair climb. If the Nongriat trek isn't right for everyone in your group, Riwai lets you still see a living root bridge up close.

Mawlynnong itself is widely known as one of Asia's cleanest villages, with tidy bamboo-fenced lanes and a bamboo Sky View Tower you can climb for a view over the forest towards Bangladesh. It sits on the way between Cherrapunji and Dawki, so it fits naturally into Day 5.

## Dawki and the Umngot river

Dawki sits right at the India–Bangladesh border, and its claim to fame is the Umngot river — water so clear that boats drifting across it genuinely look like they're floating in mid-air over the riverbed. This clarity is at its best from November to February, when there's little rain to cloud the water; in the monsoon the river runs fast and muddy instead.

Boats can be hired at the main Dawki boating point or further upstream at Shnongpdeng, a quieter stretch of the same river that's also popular for riverside camping — tents and bamboo cottages are available if you want a night by the water instead of in a hotel. Because you're near an international border, carry a valid photo ID with you at all times here; ID checks are more likely in this stretch than elsewhere in the state.

## Documents, permits and local rules to know before you go

Indian citizens do not need an Inner Line Permit (ILP) for Meghalaya — unlike neighbouring Arunachal Pradesh, Nagaland, Mizoram and Manipur, the state has no ILP requirement, as confirmed on the official Meghalaya tourism website. You should still carry a valid government photo ID at all times — Aadhaar, passport, driving licence or voter ID are all accepted — and keep it handy near Dawki, where checks are more common given the border.

Within East Khasi Hills district, which covers Sohra/Cherrapunji and the Nongriat trek, a registered local guide is required for treks and outdoor activities. Confirm the current position with your hotel or driver before the trek — rules like these are enforced locally and can be checked on the spot.

Meghalaya has also made it mandatory for homestays, resorts and private hosts across the state — not just in East Khasi Hills — to register every guest through the state tourism app, linked to local police records. Carry ID and expect this at check-in anywhere in Meghalaya.

Meghalaya has also been reported to be piloting a wider visitor-registration system at some entry points, starting near Umling in Ri-Bhoi district. This is still being rolled out rather than a fixed, statewide rule at the time of writing, so ask your hotel or driver whether it affects the route you're taking.
