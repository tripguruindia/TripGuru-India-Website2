Dubai packs a 300-year-old trading creek, the world's tallest building, a working desert and a cluster of theme parks into one city - which is exactly why it suits families where a toddler, a teenager and the grandparents all want something different. The trick is sequencing the hot, outdoor days (desert, souks, gardens) against the indoor or evening ones (malls, aquariums, the Fountain) so nobody is wiped out by day three.

## How many days do you need in Dubai with family?

Four to five days is the sweet spot for a first family trip. Three days works if you only want the headline sights - Burj Khalifa, one theme park, one desert safari - but you'll be moving fast. Five days lets you add Old Dubai at a relaxed pace, a second theme park or waterpark, and a buffer morning for the pool when the heat or the kids demand it.

A shape that works for most families:

- **Day 1** - arrive, settle in, an easy evening near Dubai Mall or your hotel
- **Day 2** - Old Dubai in the morning (before the heat builds), rest in the afternoon, Burj Khalifa and the Dubai Fountain in the evening
- **Day 3** - desert safari, morning or evening depending on your children's ages
- **Day 4** - Miracle Garden and/or Global Village, or a full theme park day
- **Day 5** - Ski Dubai, a waterpark, or the Dubai Frame, then departure

Dubai is well connected by air from Delhi, Mumbai and most other major Indian cities - check the airline sites or [tripguru.com/destinations/dubai](/destinations/dubai) when you're ready to compare routes and fares.

## Old Dubai: the creek, the souks and the abra crossing

This is the part of the trip that feels like a different country from the glass towers of Downtown. Dubai Creek splits historic Dubai in two: the Bur Dubai side has the Al Fahidi Historic District, a knot of sandstone lanes and wind-towers that's free to walk and open round the clock; the Deira side has the Gold Souk (350-plus retailers under one roof) and the Spice Souk, trading spices, dried fruit, nuts and perfume since the late 1800s.

The two sides are connected by the **abra**, a traditional wooden ferry that's been crossing the creek for generations. It runs continuously, costs AED 1 per person (cash only, about ₹26), and the five-minute ride across the water - dhows moored along the bank, the gold shop windows catching the light on one side - is as memorable for most visitors as anything you pay for on the trip.

Old Dubai suits couples and anyone curious about the city's trading history more than it suits very young children, who can tire of narrow lanes and shopping fast. But the abra ride itself, the sparkle of the Gold Souk windows, and the smell of the Spice Souk's open sacks of cardamom and saffron tend to land well with kids aged seven or eight and up. Go in the morning, before the heat sets in, and keep it to two or three hours. Some of the small heritage museums inside Al Fahidi occasionally close for renovation or maintenance - check locally on the day rather than building your plan around a specific one.

## Burj Khalifa and the Dubai Frame: which is worth it for your family?

**Burj Khalifa** has two ticket tiers: "At the Top" takes you to levels 124 and 125, while "At the Top SKY" adds level 148 for a higher, pricier view. Current ticket listings put non-prime time slots for the standard tier at around AED 173 (about ₹4,530) per adult, sunset/prime slots at around AED 250 (about ₹6,550), and the SKY tier at roughly AED 400-554 (about ₹10,480-14,520). These change with season and promotions, so always confirm the current price on the official Burj Khalifa site (burjkhalifa.ae) before booking.

Below the tower, the **Dubai Fountain** is free to watch from the Waterfront Promenade or the quieter Souk Al Bahar side - evening shows run every 30 minutes from 6pm to 11pm, with seasonal lunchtime shows around 1pm (not during Ramadan). For most families this free show, timed either side of dinner, is worth building the evening around.

The **Dubai Frame** in Zabeel Park is the budget-friendlier alternative: a 150-metre picture-frame structure that lets you look at Old Dubai from one side and the new skyline from the other in a single stop. Adult tickets run roughly AED 52.5-75 (about ₹1,375-1,965) and child tickets around AED 25 (about ₹655) - a fraction of Burj Khalifa's cost for a comparable "look at the whole city" experience, which makes it a sensible pick if you're choosing one view over the other, or doing both on a longer trip.

## Desert safari with kids: what to expect, what to check

A standard desert safari combines 4x4 dune bashing, a camel ride, sandboarding, henna application, and a BBQ dinner at a desert camp with live entertainment such as Tanoura dance and a fire show. It's the one day most families remember most - the dunes turning orange at sunset, the quiet once the engines stop, dinner under a sky with far more stars than most of India's cities show.

The part to check carefully is dune bashing with small children. There is no industry-wide minimum age for dune bashing, and policies vary widely by operator - some will take infants if held by a family member, while others set a minimum of 6, 10 or even 12 for the dune-bashing segment specifically. UAE media have reported fatal accidents involving unbelted small children sitting on an adult's lap during dune bashing, so this is worth taking seriously rather than treating as a formality. Ask the operator directly whether your child will be in an approved, belted child seat during the bashing segment, and request a slower drive straight to camp as an alternative if you're not fully satisfied with the answer.

## Miracle Garden, Global Village and the theme park day

**Dubai Miracle Garden** - flower sculptures and arches across a huge open garden - opens 9am to 9pm daily, with last entry around 8.30pm and most visits capped at about three hours, which is about right before the heat and the crowds both build. Tourist tickets for Season 15 run around AED 100 for adults and AED 90 for children (roughly ₹2,620 and ₹2,360), with children under 3 free. It photographs beautifully and works for every age, including couples who just want a pretty hour outdoors.

**Global Village** is a seasonal cultural and amusement park with pavilions, rides, street food and shows, running roughly October to May each year. It opens from 4pm, staying open to midnight Sunday-Wednesday and to 1am Thursday-Saturday and on public holidays. Tickets run about AED 25 on weekdays and AED 30 on weekends (roughly ₹655 and ₹785) - an easy, affordable evening out that suits families with kids of all ages.

For a dedicated **theme park day**, Legoland Dubai and Motiongate Dubai sit together at Dubai Parks and Resorts; IMG Worlds of Adventure is a separate, standalone indoor park at City of Arabia, several kilometres away. Pick one and go deep rather than trying to combine them in a day - they each take most of a day on their own. IMG offers free entry for children up to 1.05m tall; Legoland (roughly AED 295 online, about ₹7,730) suits kids roughly 5-12 best; Motiongate starts from around AED 295 and leans slightly older with its movie-themed rides.

A few wildcards worth knowing about: **Ski Dubai**, the indoor snow park at Mall of the Emirates (roughly $69-89, about ₹6,630-8,570), is a good escape on a very hot day or if someone in the group just wants to see snow. **Wild Wadi Water Park** near Burj Al Arab and **Atlantis Aquaventure** on Palm Jumeirah (the region's largest waterpark) both work well for active kids and teens. And the **Dubai Aquarium & Underwater Zoo** inside Dubai Mall (adult around AED 120, child around AED 95 - about ₹3,145 and ₹2,490) is an easy add-on if you're already shopping or sheltering from the heat there.

## Visa and documents for an Indian family

Indians do not get blanket visa-free entry to Dubai. There are two routes:

1. **Visa-on-arrival** - available only if you hold a valid US visit visa, a US green card, or a UK/EU residence visa, each with at least six months' validity remaining. This lets you stay up to 14 days, extendable by another 14 days on payment of a fee.
2. **Pre-arranged tourist e-visa** - the route most ordinary passport holders need, applied for in advance through an airline (such as Emirates, Etihad, flydubai or Air Arabia), your hotel, a licensed travel agent, or the official ICP (icp.gov.ae) / GDRFA Dubai (gdrfad.gov.ae) portals. Standard processing takes about 2-4 working days.

Note that holding GCC residency (say, if you work in Saudi Arabia or Qatar) does not itself qualify you for visa-on-arrival - you'll still need to arrange a visa.

Keep your passport valid for at least six months from your date of entry, and have on hand: the passport bio page and front cover, a white-background photo, a confirmed return flight, your hotel booking, and recent bank statements. Every child needs their own visa document too - there's no "children travel free" exemption - and some visa routes may ask for an English copy of the child's birth certificate, so confirm the exact document list with your airline or agent when you apply. Visa fee figures quoted by private visa-agent websites vary and aren't official GDRFA/ICP figures, so treat any number you see online as indicative and apply through an authorised airline, agent or the official portals for the current fee.

Before you travel, check the latest advisory from India's Ministry of External Affairs ([mea.gov.in](https://www.mea.gov.in)) and the Indian Embassy in the UAE.
